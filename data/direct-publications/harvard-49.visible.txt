Abstract
We present a cross-disciplinary approach to quantitatively evaluate the suitability of underground hydrogen storage (UHS) in hydrocarbon fields within the Los Angeles Basin, California. GIS mapping tools were applied to existing datasets from the California Geologic Energy Management Division, the U.S. Geoscience Information Network, and other open-sources to obtain quantitative measures of reservoir performance as well as infrastructure proximity and population density. We then applied the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) in a three-tier hierarchy for this multi-criteria selection process. The AHP ranks each of the 67 subject fields in the basin against the study group according to performance in geologic, socioeconomic, and risk-driven criteria. Systematic pairwise comparisons across the complete set are used to derive weighted scores for UHS suitability, with weights informed from existing literature. The methodology results in comparative statements about relative reservoir suitability for UHS development and appears robust to uncertainties in weight setting. The method identifies the highest ranked fields as having large potential storage volumes, strong reservoir performance, and locations in more stable tectonic settings close to potential hydrogen production or transport infrastructure. In particular, El Segundo, Inglewood, Dominguez, Wilmington, and Playa Del Rey are identified as top performing candidates for UHS. The method, which is generally applicable and both computationally efficient and flexible, can be adjusted to consider alternative weighting of economic and environmental risk factors. This allows identifying top candidate fields for more specific reservoir engineering analysis of UHS suitability.

Introduction
Global climate challenges and the threat they pose to modern societies have become central to both scientific and public discourse over the past few decades. A critical component in finding solutions to these challenges is addressing the oversized role carbon-heavy energy sources play in our current infrastructure [1], [2], [3], [4]. Following the 2021 United Nations Climate Change Conference (COP26), further climate goals were added with the EU collectively declaring that its energy mix will comprise of 55% renewable energy sources (RES) by 2050 [5]. The United States also pledged to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by 50%–52% below 2005 levels by 2030, with this target being part of a broader commitment to achieve net-zero emissions by 2050 [6]. In line with these goals, on October 13, 2023, it was announced that California would receive up to $1.2 billion from the US Department of Energy to accelerate the development and deployment of renewable hydrogen programs across the state [7]. As one of the largest residential and industrial energy markets in the world, using hydrogen to make a meaningful impact on the carbon emissions from energy and transport in the Los Angeles region requires large scale infrastructural investment [8].
Currently the primary technological barriers for further adoption of RES in the global energy mix are storage limitations, atmospheric fluctuation in the environmental factors that determine renewable energy production, and changing energy demands that create mismatches in the supply–demand equation [2], [9], [10]. In hydrocarbon driven energy production, scaling up and down to match demand is well understood. However, RES requires complementary solutions to achieve the same flexibility [11], [12]. Currently, most large-scale energy storage is operated through rechargeable applications such as Pumped Hydroelectric Storage (PHS) and battery array storage, but the geographic restrictions of the former and the scale issues of the latter demand alternative solutions [13], [14]. UHS offers a way to provide complementary support to renewable energy systems and fill a role in the energy supply typically occupied by carbon emitting alternatives.
Research into UHS in geological structures is currently focused on salt domes, saline aquifers, and depleted oil and gas fields [2], [15]. In full applications salt domes are the most common, with significant effort going into testing aquifers and oil and gas fields for UHS suitability [16], [17], [18], [19]. The advantage over current hydrogen storage methods in salt domes offered by aquifers and depleted oil and gas fields lies in the greatly increased scale of storage [20]. The economics of scale moves hydrogen as an energy storage method closer to the economic viability it needs to achieve to become a significant factor in decarbonizing the energy sector [21].
Given the lithology and structural features present in the subsurface of the basin, coupled with the extensive geological data available from decades of oil and gas exploration, the Los Angeles basin appears to be uniquely suited for research into UHS development in its depleted oil and gas reservoirs [22]. The concept of a ‘storage play’ as related to the hydrocarbon industry term ‘petroleum play’[23] is used by Heinemann et al. [14] in the context of hydrogen storage to define a reservoir with suitable volume, porosity, permeability, seal and trap structure for safe hydrogen storage. This term is adopted throughout this study in the context of potential UHS plays.
Existing screening techniques for prospective UHS plays are limited, especially considering evaluation on the basin scale. Hurtado et al. [19] explored a qualitative framework considering Health, Safety and Environment (HSE) risk factors, evaluating criteria on a scale from 2 to −2. An extensive selection of HSE risk factors were considered, including seal thickness, lithology, permeability and trapping mechanisms. These risk factors were grouped into categories like primary seals and trapping mechanisms. As part of the evaluation the level of certainty in a data source is also considered quantitatively, providing coefficients to the assigned scores. The methodology is then applied to two target reservoirs on the Iberian peninsula, identifying comparative differences between the two from their performance in each category. The paper emphasizes that HSE risks associated with UHS must be accurately estimated in order to provide decision-makers with the necessary tools to identify factors that may interfere with a project and to allow effective communication with public stakeholders.
Harati et al. [24] suggest an evaluation of a reduced number of key criteria in a multi-criteria site selection methodology. They apply a combined AHP and PROMETHEE – a decision making structure utilizing preference functions – framework to consider geologic and economic factors for UHS in the UK. The study targeted only “gas fields”, hydrocarbon fields containing dry and/or wet gas. Gas condensate and gas cap-bearing oil fields were not considered, as were reservoirs with active aquifer support. In total, 71 total gas reservoirs were considered. Optimal values for assessed criteria are explored, such as net-to-gross ratio, porosity and permeability. The authors emphasize that final decisions on whether risk factors such as a faulted trap structure detract significantly from overall suitability must be determined in a thorough case-by-case analysis of the geomechanical characteristics of each site. Among the assessed criteria, the paper determined that maximum well deliverability rate was the most important factor in UHS site selection for depleted gas reservoirs, with the Cygnus field being the top candidate for UHS plays in the study group.
Nemati et al. [25] present a Fuzzy-Delphi methodology to assign weights to the criteria umbrellas of Technical, Economics, HSE, and Social as well as the sub-criteria within. The authors extend the list of considered criteria to 18, including purely social factors such as social acceptance for a project and the local culture around a site. In their criteria weightings, legal restrictions were identified as the most important for site evaluation, outranking fluid flow aspects like reservoir permeability and porosity. The contribution was purely in the methodology and criteria rankings as the method was not applied to any specific reservoirs for evaluation. Instead the authors demonstrated the priority of factors that could be considered.
Lewandowska-Śmierzchalska et al. [26] approach site selection from a multi-criteria decision making (MCDM) angle. They apply the Analytic Hierarchy Process to 6 selected geomechanical criteria in the case of salt or hydrocarbon deposits or 5 for aquifers. The paper assesses the validity of the AHP method and details the use of both qualitative and quantitative measures in its selection criteria. Additionally they explain and explore the 1–9 Saaty scale outlined in the foundational AHP paper [27]. Through the course of the study the authors demonstrate the efficacy of developed decision-making structures in ranking individual geological structures.
Okoroafor et al. [28] discuss hydrogen’s role in the energy sector, alongside an extensive review of similar or related works in the UHS space. The study focuses on depleted reservoirs in Northern California for potential storage sites, outlining a three stage site selection workflow for prospective UHS plays: site screening, site ranking, and site characterization. The screening criteria cover three umbrella categories, storage and withdrawal optimization, risk and hydrogen loss minimization, and environment and economic considerations. These groupings outline minimum or maximum values for criteria beyond which the site is discarded for further consideration in UHS development. For site ranking the paper explores a qualitative ranking between 1–5 for each assessed criteria. Catchment buckets are delineated for values of a given criteria, and then used to assign qualitative scores to the entire study group. In their results, 35 reservoirs passed the stage 1 screening, with those being ranked according to seven key parameters. The paper highlights the need for current and accurate values for parameters when performing screening and ranking for potential UHS sites, and demonstrates the value of a numerical reservoir model in site evaluations.
Other studies in related areas include examinations of screening criteria [17], GIS based analysis of salt caverns [29], regional applications of the AHP methodology [30], feasibility of hydrocarbon sites for UHS [31], and methodologies for identifying assessment criteria [32].
Given the existing lithologies, geological structures, and its history of oil and gas exploration, the target region of the Los Angeles basin shown in Fig. 1 offers many unique opportunities and challenges [22]. The primary aim of the study is to develop a holistic evaluation framework for development of potential UHS plays in depleted hydrocarbon fields, using it to guide the initial site-selection phase of hydrogen energy development in the Los Angeles region. The data used in this project is primarily quantitative, and includes oil well logs, reservoir compendiums, technical papers and reports from the Los Angeles region published between 1974 and 2023 (see supplementary material for full sub-criteria spreadsheets). Our approach aims to integrate both site characteristics and uncertainty where datasets are incomplete to create a comparative ranking of the relative suitability each field holds for development as an UHS play. This is achieved by applying the Analytic Hierarchy process to the multi-criteria decision making problem posed by the extensive datasets, allowing us to generate a relative ranking for UHS suitability by reservoir. The conclusions from the application of the methodology provide valuable direction for further field specific research and infrastructural investment in the Los Angeles region and other similar sites worldwide.

Methodology
The methodology we adopted to select candidate fields 1 for UHS within the Los Angeles Basin is an application of the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) [27], [33]. We consider high-level geological and non-geological factors of reservoirs to create a ranking of their potential UHS suitability. The AHP is a broadly implemented MCDM tool that facilitates a decomposition of the problem into distinct criteria, which can be evaluated and aggregated to create an overall ranking of potential solutions.
