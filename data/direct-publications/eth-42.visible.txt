Enhancing high temperature heat transfer for clean industrial process heating via radiatively participating gases
Madelyn Bennett, Vincent M. Wheeler, Trevor Wilson, Robert Anderson, William Bierzychudek, Dragan Mejic, Kent Warren and Alan W. Weimer
Applied Thermal Engineering, vol. 306, pp. 133157, Elsevier, 2026.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.applthermaleng.2026.133157
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

High-temperature industrial heat demand represents a major source of CO2 emissions. Solar receivers in concentrated solar technology (CST) systems have reached temperatures exceeding 1000 °C, offering a pathway for reducing carbon emissions from high-temperature industrial processes. At these temperatures, thermal radiation becomes an important mode of heat transfer, yet no experimental study has compared radiatively participating (CO2) and non-participating (N2) gases as heat transfer fluids (HTFs), a gap this work addresses with the first such experimental comparison, to the author's knowledge, from 1000 °C to 1400 °C. A high-temperature experimental setup was designed and constructed to measure centerline temperature profiles via an axially driven Type R thermocouple for different CO2 and N2 flow rates. Experimental measurements validated the computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model, which was used to investigate the effects of pipe diameters and Reynolds numbers. Increasing Reynolds numbers showed slightly decreased radiation contribution for a given geometry. Increasing the pipe diameter from 10 mm to 100 mm increased the optical thickness from 0.0405 to 0.405 for the 2.7 μm band and 0.0648 to 0.648 for the 4.3 μm band, leading to stronger radiation absorption for CO2. The radiation contribution to the overall heat flux increased from 3.5% to 66.6% for 10 mm to 100 mm pipe diameters. Larger pipe diameters showed significant radiation absorption for CO2, allowing CO2 to carry more heat than N2 on a mass flow rate basis. These findings demonstrate the potential of radiatively participating gases to improve heat transfer performance in high-temperature industrial processes and CST systems.

Particle atomic layer deposition in fluidized bed reactors: An updated perspective on reactor design and low-pressure fluidization
Davis R. Conklin, Hailey C. Loehde-Woolard, Julie A. Nguyen, Michael A. Zaza, Bergen Evans, William M. Paulson, Hermann F. Klein-Hessling Barrientos, Mark Wallace, Robert L. Anderson, Dragan Mejic, Kent Warren and Alan W. Weimer
Powder Technology, vol. 476, pp. 122364, Elsevier, 2026.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.powtec.2026.122364
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Atomic layer deposition (ALD) on particle substrates has become widely utilized over the last two decades. The ALD fluidized bed reactor (ALD-FBR) is a reactor design popularized by work at the University of Colorado; their work with lab-scale reactor systems has produced fundamental learnings for the field, including the capability of ALD to coat primary nanoparticles. As the applications of particle ALD continue to expand, so do the complexity of ALD chemistry and powder processing. To maintain a high level of knowledge and reproducibility across systems, we detail several aspects of ALD-FBRs that have achieved success with challenging precursor or substrate requirements and/or particle fluidization characteristics. Here we discuss considerations for precursor manifolds, precursor introductions to the bed, and air-sensitive materials. In addition, practices for particle fluidization under low pressure (<130 hPa) are discussed with a focus on cohesive powders.

Three-dimensional porous carbons via direct ink writing of bio-sourced precursors, thermal processing, and response surface methodology
Katarina Odak, Kent Warren, Nathan A. Smith, Adrian Gestos, James E. Smay, David E. Clough and Alan W. Weimer
Carbon Trends, vol. 23, pp. 100654, Elsevier, 2026.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.cartre.2026.100654
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

This study presents a manufacturing and processing route for fabricating CO₂-activated three-dimensional porous carbon (3D-PC) materials from microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) and kraft lignin (L), leveraging direct ink writing and controlled thermal processing. Response surface methodology (RSM) was employed to optimize ink formulations based on print shape fidelity, assessed via structured-light 3D scanning in an alternative, high-throughput approach to conventional rheological testing. Printed lignocellulosic structures underwent a three-day thermal schedule under inert conditions, with extended dwell in the 100 260 C range to promote cross-linking of the thermolabile lignin component and char formation from the cellulosic fraction. This process yielded porous carbon architectures with developed internal surface area and sufficient handling robustness for post-processing. A second RSM framework was applied to fine-tune CO₂ activation parameters, enhancing microporosity through selective carbon removal via the Boudouard reaction at defect sites. Nitrogen adsorption isotherms (196 C) confirmed the development of micropores (< 2 nm) without significant geometric degradation, indicating controlled activation rather than unselective gasification. Cold field-emission scanning electron microscopy (CFE-SEM) revealed interconnected meso/macroporosity (> 2 nm), while atomic force microscopy (AFM) confirmed sub-nanometer surface features. This study presents an integrated approach for producing high surface-area porous carbons from simple, bio-sourced, water-compatible precursors through direct ink writing, carbonization, and CO₂ activation, supporting the scalable design of architected porous carbon materials and establishing a manufacturing framework relevant to applications such as energy storage, gas adsorption, thermal insulation, and catalysis.

Cation Vacancies Mediate Thermochemical Water Splitting with Iron Aluminates
Nathan J. Szymanski, Kent Warren, Alan W. Weimer and Christopher J. Bartel
Chemistry of Materials, vol. 38: no. 7, pp. 3145-3156, American Chemical Society, 2026.
external page:
DOI: 10.1021/acs.chemmater.5c02365
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Solar thermochemical water splitting enables hydrogen production by cycling metal oxides between reduced and oxidized states, typically through an oxygen vacancy mechanism. However, recent experimental work suggests that cation vacancies have a greater influence on the redox behavior of iron aluminate spinels used in water splitting. This remains debated, as calculations predict that such cation vacancies are thermodynamically unfavorable. In the current work, we show that Fe vacancies in (FeζAl₁−ζ)₃O₄ become accessible only when facilitated by inversion between Fe and Al. This antisite disorder lowers the formation energy of octahedral Fe vacancies in Al-rich spinels (ζ = 1/3) from over 3 eV to just 0.62 eV when one-third of the cation sites are inverted, allowing high Fe vacancy concentrations under oxidizing conditions. This mechanism supports high H₂ yields up to 361 μmol/g, consistent with experimental observations. Our findings support the notion that solar thermochemical water splitting can occur through a cation vacancy mechanism. They also clarify how site inversion, vacancy energetics, and defect interactions each contribute to redox performance, offering general design principles for identifying and optimizing materials that operate through cation vacancy cycling.

Accelerating activated sintering of tungsten with palladium particle atomic layer deposition
Davis R. Conklin, Hailey C. Loehde-Woolard, Michael Zaza, Bergen Evans, Arne Croell, Martin Volz, Kent Warren, Rishi Raj, Jhonathan Rosales and Alan W. Weimer
International Journal of Refractory Metals & Hard Materials, vol. 135, pp. 107554, Elsevier, 2026.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.ijrmhm.2025.107554
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Tungsten refractory metal parts are produced by sintering metal powder compacts well above 2000 °C. Transition metal additives (e.g. Ni, Pd) can be deposited on the surfaces of the tungsten particles prior to sintering to create a sub-nanometer intergranular film that accelerates diffusional mass transport and resulting sintering kinetics to allow sintering below 1500 °C. The uniformity of the additives is known to influence sintering performance, but prior sintering aid studies for tungsten utilize solution impregnation techniques that limit control over additive distribution. In this work, we employ particle atomic layer deposition (ALD) to deposit a highly uniform Pd coating on tungsten powder and demonstrate improved activated sintering kinetics relative to Pd deposited by solution impregnation. Both techniques were used to deposit about 0.1 wt% Pd, but an increase in powder surface area during the solution impregnation process reduced the surface-specific Pd loading relative to ALD-coated tungsten. The ALD technique also reduced oxygen and carbon contamination and produced superior Pd dispersion. As a result, the ALD Pd-W powder began sintering about 100 °C below the impregnated powder and exhibited a lower activation energy for densification. Although this produces minor microstructural differences in powders sintered under typical hours-long heating cycles, we anticipate that this approach will enable shorter, cooler, and ultimately less expensive processes for tungsten powder metallurgy. This is the first application of particle ALD for powder metallurgy, demonstrating a promising approach to incorporate dilute additives in instances where uniformity and composition control are critical to processing and performance.

Technoeconomic analysis of the modular valorization of flare gas to carbon additives for concrete
Jessica A. Hauck, Theodore A. Champ, Kent Warren, Andrew Broerman, Boning Wang, Mija H. Hubler and Alan W. Weimer
Energy Conversion and Management, vol. 348, pp. 120783, Elsevier, 2026.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.enconman.2025.120783
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Despite widespread efforts to mitigate emissions, significant volumes of natural gas continue to be flared due to limited infrastructure and the absence of mobile systems for on-site utilization. A promising approach is a modular system designed to valorize flare gas by converting natural gas into carbon nanomaterials for use in concrete. Unlike conventional methane pyrolysis which prioritizes hydrogen as the primary product, this design proposes using hydrogen as a heat source and instead targets carbon, the dominant product by mass, as the economic driver. The system employs a fluidized bed chemical vapor deposition reactor with a sacrificial catalyst composed of iron deposited onto silica fume. This process yields a composite carbon nanoproduct containing carbon nanoparticles and nanofilaments deposited on the catalyst, eliminating the need for product separation or catalyst regeneration. The modular unit is designed for remote deployment and includes integrated sulfur removal, requiring only natural gas as the thermal input. Technoeconomic analysis shows that the levelized selling price of the solid product remains below $7 per kilogram across a range of catalyst loadings and carbon yields, with carbon nanoproduct values ranging from $14 to $62 per kilogram, not including the cost of carbon–catalyst separation. The product can be incorporated into cement at 0.25 wt% loading with less than a 10 % increase in cost, enabling applications in high-performance concrete. While further experimental validation and design optimization are warranted, this study demonstrates a novel and viable concept for converting flared gas into functional carbon additives for concrete.

Design of laboratory-scale systems for thermochemical water splitting
Julie A. Nguyen and Kent Warren
Frontiers in Energy Research, vol. 14, pp. 1801118, Frontiers Media, 2026.
external page:
DOI: 10.3389/fenrg.2026.1801118
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Thermochemical water splitting has emerged as a promising pathway for sustainable hydrogen production, offering a theoretically efficient and inherently scalable route for converting renewable heat into chemical fuel. While significant effort has been devoted to the discovery of redox-active materials, comparatively less attention has been given to evaluating how material performance changes once architectures or reactor configurations amenable to further scale-up are considered. Bridging the gap between benchtop analytical characterization efforts and a fully integrated pilot demonstration requires the development of dedicated experimental platforms capable of reproducing the conditions expected in practical operation, all without compromising insight into the fundamental phenomena that govern thermochemical reactions. To this end, we outline key, yet rarely reported, considerations associated with the design and application of such laboratory-scale systems for advancing thermochemical water-splitting technologies. The discussion encompasses methods for ensuring precise conditioning of inlet gases, a proven design strategy for accommodating both high-temperature and high-pressure reactor operation, and protocols for mitigating common experimental artifacts. In addition, how operating conditions influence the thermodynamic driving force for oxygen exchange in metal oxides is examined. Finally, a complete workflowfrom raw analyzer signals to processed measurements with quantified uncertaintyis provided to illustrate best practices for data interpretation and dissemination. Although presented in the context of thermochemical water splitting, the methodologies described herein are broadly applicable to the experimental investigation of heterogeneous thermochemical processes.

Method of Quantified Mass Spectrometry for Atomic Layer Deposition Fluidized Bed Reactors
Julie A. Nguyen, Kent Warren, Jaime W. DuMont, Davis R. Conklin, William M. Paulson, Dragan Mejic and Alan W. Weimer
Chemistry of Materials, vol. 37: no. 24, pp. 9633-9645, American Chemical Society, 2025.
external page:
DOI: 10.1021/acs.chemmater.5c02268
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Mass spectrometers have been used in-line on atomic layer deposition fluidized bed reactors (ALD-FBRs) to track the progress of the ALD reaction. However, the use of mass spectrometry or, more specifically, residual gas analysis for ALD-FBRs has been limited to qualitative measurements indicating the appearance and removal of ALD precursor and byproduct species. Here we provide a method to perform in situ gas species quantification using a residual gas analyzer (RGA) with an ALD-FBR. We explain how to propagate error sources across the ALD-FBR system to the final quantified mass value. Additionally, we present the first example of using an RGA to quantify in situ a reaction byproduct in an ALD-FBR. The trimethylaluminum (TMA)/water chemistry was conducted on single crystal LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 (NMC811) powder and the carbon dioxide byproduct was quantified for each ALD half reaction. The RGA quantification method presented in this work allows in situ surface characterization of the ALD reaction on a powder substrate. We envision ALD-FBR operators will be able to use the method for their own ALD chemistries and substrates, especially during the initial ALD cycles where the ALD precursors interact with the substrate surface and reactions besides the expected ALD reaction are likely to occur.

A phenomenological model for cellulose charring during low temperature pyrolysis
Katarina Odak, Kent Warren, Nathan A. Smith and Alan W. Weimer
Journal of Analytical and Applied Pyrolysis, vol. 192, pp. 107330, Elsevier, 2025.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.jaap.2025.107330
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Cellulose pyrolysis produces solids (char), liquids (tar and bio-oil), and gases in fractions depending on experimental conditions. Of which, the transformation of cellulose to highly conjugated, carbon-based macromolecules of char is still not understood. Several authors have attributed elevated char yields to low temperatures (below 300°C) and conditions like large sample sizes or high pressures, as well as the presence of a furan-type intermediate in the carbonization of cellulose. Key studies on cellulose, glucose, and levoglucosan pyrolysis are reviewed here to offer a perspective on the charring of cellulose during low temperature pyrolysis, explaining how conditions limiting the bulk diffusion of water and other endogenous acids out of the charring matrix during low-temperature treatment (below 300°C) promote the formation of a thermostable furan-type intermediate that carbonizes more readily than it devolatilizes, ultimately enhancing the final char yield after heating to high temperatures (to and above 700°C). Thermogravimetric experiments with in-line mass spectrometry of evolved gases, paired with Raman spectroscopy and elemental analysis of the solid residue after low- and hightemperature treatment, are used for model support. A phenomenological understanding of cellulose transformation at the nanometer-scale, from packed saccharide chains to highly conjugated carbon macromolecules, is offered to advance the understanding of how cellulosic fibers or particles retain macroscopic geometries during pyrolysis. More broadly, this perspective on cellulose charring during low-temperature pyrolysis aligns with observations from other transformation pathways to contribute to a unified understanding of cellulose thermochemical conversion, which is increasingly relevant for selective carbonization, bio-oil production, and platform chemical synthesis.

Preparation of homogeneous W Re alloys by coating tungsten powder with perrhenic acid
Davis R. Conklin, Hermann F. Klein-Hessling Barrientos, Arne Croell, Jhonathan Rosales, Kent Warren and Alan W. Weimer
International Journal of Refractory Metals & Hard Materials, vol. 132, pp. 107285, Elsevier, 2025.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.ijrmhm.2025.107285
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Tungsten‑rhenium (Wsingle bondRe) solid solution alloys provide a unique combination of mechanical and thermal properties that are utilized in many high-temperature applications. A homogeneous rhenium concentration is crucial for mechanical performance, but this is difficult to achieve in near-net-shape manufacturing without employing specialized powder preparation and consolidation techniques. Here we show that coating tungsten powder with a perrhenic acid solution substantially improves alloy homogeneity by reducing diffusion distances and promoting pre-alloying relative to metal powder mixtures. This strengthens the rhenium softening effect to produce superior Wsingle bondRe alloys with conventional, commercially relevant powder metallurgy techniques.

Tuning transition metal nanoparticles on a non-traditional support via experimental design
Jessica A. Hauck, Kent Warren, Samantha Harshberger, Luis Kitsu Iglesias, Katarina Odak, Nathan Smith, Guillermo Rojas, Christopher Gump, Michael F. Toney and Alan W. Weimer
Applied Catalysis A: General, vol. 706, pp. 120464, Elsevier, 2025.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.apcata.2025.120464
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

The ability to control metal nanoparticle size and morphology on supported catalysts is crucial for optimizing catalytic performance in targeted applications. This work presents a systematic approach for tuning Ni particle and crystallite size on an unconventional, low-porosity silica fume support through select thermal treatments. The catalyst was synthesized via the deposition of nickelocene onto silica fume, resulting in well-dispersed Ni nanoparticles. A face-centered central composite design was employed to systematically assess the effects of time, temperature, and sintering gas environment on metal particle growth. The results demonstrate that the sintering gas environment is the primary factor governing particle and crystallite evolution, with temperature as the next most significant influence. Nickel nanoparticles sintered at temperatures of 650 °C and above under inert conditions exhibited substantial growth and polycrystalline structures, whereas samples treated in oxidative environments formed NiO, restricting particle mobility. Minimally oxidative (500 ppm O₂) environments facilitated rapid sintering while effectively removing residual ligands from the one-step nickelocene deposition process. Extensive structural characterization via a combination of scanning transmission electron microscopy, Xray diffraction, hydrogen temperature programmed reduction, and small-angle X-ray scattering revealed that oxidative treatments enhanced metal-support interactions, as evidenced by increased reduction temperatures and narrower particle size distributions. These findings establish quantitative relationships between sintering parameters and Ni nanoparticle characteristics, providing a framework for rational catalyst design through controlled thermal treatments. This methodology is broadly applicable to other catalytic systems and provides a quantitative foundation for catalyst design.

Fabrication and Characterization of a Reticulated Porous Iron Aluminate Ceramic for Solar Thermal Water and Carbon Dioxide Splitting
Linnea Helenius, Catherine Trautman, Jess Connell, William Paulson, Alice Ly, Kent Warren and Alan Weimer
Materials Science and Technology Technical Meeting and Exhibition (MS&T26), Pittsburgh, PA, USA, October 4-7, 2026.
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Reticulated porous ceramic (RPC) iron aluminate (Fe0.5Al0.5)3O4 parts are created as an active material to thermochemically split water and carbon dioxide. The RPC facilitates this high temperature (1400°C-1600°C) two-step redox cycle by increasing the specific surface area to promote reaction rates and increasing the porosity to allow faster heat transfer by radiation. It has advantages over particle-based reactor designs because it is less prone to channeling and elutriation or diffusion of hot particles to heat-sensitive reactor components. The RPC structures are fabricated via the replication method. A polyurethane foam is cast in iron aluminate slurry and subjected to a thermal treatment to burn out the foam and sinter the part. The morphology of the RPC is characterized by micro-computed tomography, scanning electron microscopy, mercury porosimetry, image analysis, and hydrodynamic experiments to elucidate the specific surface area, porosity, pore diameter, strut diameter, and permeability. Thermochemical performance is evaluated using thermogravimetry.

Ceria and Iron Aluminate Active Materials for Solar Thermochemical Fuel Production
Kent Warren
10th Forum on New Materials (CIMTEC 2026), Perugia, Italy Faenza: CIMTEC 2026, June 21-24, 2026.
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Widespread access to H2 and CO is critical for global decarbonization, yet producing a clean supply of these synthetic liquid fuel precursors at costs competitive with fossil-based alternatives remains constrained by the limited capacity and durability of incumbent electrolysis technologies. Thermochemical approaches that exploit the oxygen-exchange capacity of metal oxides offer a promising route to overcome these limitations, although challenges persist in identifying oxygen carriers capable of efficient operation within the confines of established chemical processes. In this presentation, benchmark candidates from two distinct classes of nonstoichiometric metal oxides - ceria fluorites and iron aluminate spinels - are examined for their effectiveness in facilitating thermochemical H2O and CO2 splitting. Their redox behavior, governed by oxygen- and cation-vacancy-mediated transport, respectively, is characterized through both closed- and open-system analyses to elucidate the relationship between thermodynamic properties and performance. The implications of these findings are discussed in the context of process integration, illustrating how the rational design of materials with tailored redox properties is poised to enable commercially viable routes to sustainable fuels.

Steam as an Alternative to Nitrogen Purge in Continuous High-Temperature Thermochemical Energy Storage Systems
Juvenal Ortiz-Ulloa, Zach Warren, Nick AuYeung and Kent Warren
32nd International Conference on Concentrating Solar Power, Thermal, and Chemical Energy Systems (SolarPACES 2026), Bad Neuenahr-Ahrweiler, Germany, September 15-19, 2026.
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

High-temperature thermochemical energy storage (TCES) systems commonly rely on nitrogen as an inert purge gas during reduction, incurring significant energy and cost penalties due to gas separation and recycling. This work explores steam as a cost-effective alternative, coupled with mechanical vapor recompression (MVR) to enable latent heat recovery and process integration. A combined experimental, modeling approach demonstrates that CuO-based granular media can be reduced under steam without parasitic reactions or structural degradation. System-level simulations of a 1-5 kWth reactor show that steam with MVR can reduce operating costs by over an order of magnitude compared to nitrogen systems, while maintaining competitive efficiencies. Furthermore, introducing controlled amounts of air into the steam stream (<5 mol% O₂) yields additional energy savings of ~17% by reducing vaporization demands. These results highlight steam-based purge strategies as a promising pathway for scalable, cost-efficient TCES systems integrated with concentrated solar power.

Enhancing high temperature heat transfer for clean industrial process heating via radiatively participating gases
Madelyn Bennett, Vincent M. Wheeler, Trevor Wilson, Robert Anderson, William Bierzychudek, Dragan Mejic, Kent Warren and Alan W. Weimer
Applied Thermal Engineering, vol. 306, pp. 133157, Elsevier, 2026.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.applthermaleng.2026.133157
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

High-temperature industrial heat demand represents a major source of CO2 emissions. Solar receivers in concentrated solar technology (CST) systems have reached temperatures exceeding 1000 °C, offering a pathway for reducing carbon emissions from high-temperature industrial processes. At these temperatures, thermal radiation becomes an important mode of heat transfer, yet no experimental study has compared radiatively participating (CO2) and non-participating (N2) gases as heat transfer fluids (HTFs), a gap this work addresses with the first such experimental comparison, to the author's knowledge, from 1000 °C to 1400 °C. A high-temperature experimental setup was designed and constructed to measure centerline temperature profiles via an axially driven Type R thermocouple for different CO2 and N2 flow rates. Experimental measurements validated the computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model, which was used to investigate the effects of pipe diameters and Reynolds numbers. Increasing Reynolds numbers showed slightly decreased radiation contribution for a given geometry. Increasing the pipe diameter from 10 mm to 100 mm increased the optical thickness from 0.0405 to 0.405 for the 2.7 μm band and 0.0648 to 0.648 for the 4.3 μm band, leading to stronger radiation absorption for CO2. Cation Vacancies Mediate Thermochemical Water Splitting with Iron Aluminates
Nathan J. Szymanski, Kent Warren, Alan W. Weimer and Christopher J. Bartel

external page:
DOI: 10.1021/acs.chemmater.5c02365
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Solar thermochemical water splitting enables hydrogen production by cycling metal oxides between reduced and oxidized states, typically through an oxygen vacancy mechanism. However, recent experimental work suggests that cation vacancies have a greater influence on the redox behavior of iron aluminate spinels used in water splitting. This remains debated, as calculations predict that such cation vacancies are thermodynamically unfavorable. In the current work, we show that Fe vacancies in (FeζAl₁−ζ)₃O₄ become accessible only when facilitated by inversion between Fe and Al. A phenomenological model for cellulose charring during low temperature pyrolysis
Katarina Odak, Kent Warren, Nathan A. Smith and Alan W. Weimer

external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.jaap.2025.107330
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Cellulose pyrolysis produces solids (char), liquids (tar and bio-oil), and gases in fractions depending on experimental conditions. Of which, the transformation of cellulose to highly conjugated, carbon-based macromolecules of char is still not understood. Several authors have attributed elevated char yields to low temperatures (below 300°C) and conditions like large sample sizes or high pressures, as well as the presence of a furan-type intermediate in the carbonization of cellulose. Key studies on cellulose, glucose, and levoglucosan pyrolysis are reviewed here to offer a perspective on the charring of cellulose during low temperature pyrolysis, explaining how conditions limiting the bulk diffusion of water and other endogenous acids out of the charring matrix during low-temperature treatment (below 300°C) promote the formation of a thermostable furan-type intermediate that carbonizes more readily than it devolatilizes, ultimately enhancing the final char yield after heating to high temperatures (to and above 700°C). More broadly, this perspective on cellulose charring during low-temperature pyrolysis aligns with observations from other transformation pathways to contribute to a unified understanding of cellulose thermochemical conversion, which is increasingly relevant for selective carbonization, bio-oil production, and platform chemical synthesis.

Solar thermal water splitting (STWS) produces renewable (or green) hydrogen from water using concentrated sunlight. Here, we show that this setback can be overcome with iron aluminate-based spinel solid solutions that preferentially exhibit large changes in oxygen content within the range of oxygen partial pressures expected in large-scale systems. When operated isothermally at 1400 °C, the iron aluminate-based materials demonstrate a capacity for hydrogen production greater than 500 μmol g−1 and, as a result, remain viable even under high conversion conditions (i.e., pO₂ < 500 : 1 H₂O : H₂), exceeding the hydrogen yields of ceria and two attractive perovskite candidates following a 400 °C (or less) temperature swing. Isothermal water splitting using iron aluminate-based materials opens the door for more simple, robust, and efficient production of renewable hydrogen.

Abstract 

Technologies that facilitate the conversion of CH₄ and/or CO₂ with concentrated sunlight provide a viable strategy for storing solar energy in the form of liquid fuels and reducing anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions. Facile CO₂ separation and subsequent H₂ production via chemical-looping combustion over ceria–zirconia solid solutions
Kent Warren, Caroline M. Hill, Richard J. Carrillo and Jonathan R. Scheffe
Physical Chemistry Chemical Physics, vol. 22: no. 16, pp. 8545-8556, Royal Society of Chemistry, 2020.
external page:
DOI: 10.1039/D0CP00924E
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

A novel chemical-looping combustion scheme is proposed, where facile gas separation via steam condensation enables the production of sequestrable CO₂ from alkanes, such as CH₄, and pure H₂ from H₂O. This cycle consists of two steps, namely, (1) the endothermic reduction of a ceria-based solid solution via the complete oxidation of CH₄, followed by (2) the exothermic oxidation of the reduced metal oxide via H₂O splitting. Relative to iron oxide-based materials and undoped ceria, ceria–zirconia solid solutions possess favorable partial molar enthalpic and entropic properties; this promotes selective production of complete combustion products, H₂O and CO₂, during the reforming reaction. Thermodynamic predictions suggest that the complete oxidation of CH₄ is possible by increasing the Zr content to 20 mol%, operating below 600 °C, increasing total pressure, or reducing the amount of delivered reactant. Furthermore, any H₂, CO, or unreacted CH₄ that may persist is thermodynamically favored to oxidize if exposed to unreacted oxide downstream, as is typical for a packed-bed or downer reactor configuration. Experiments were performed to validate the thermodynamic trends using isothermal thermogravimetry coupled with residual gas analysis, which confirmed that high selectivity towards H₂O and CO₂ is attainable for methane-driven reduction of Ce₀.₉Zr₀.₁O₂; selectivities greater than 0.70 were observed at initial reaction extents. Importantly, metal oxide oxidation via H₂O splitting and selective production of H₂ (or CO if CO₂ is the delivered oxidant) is also thermodynamically favored at the operating conditions considered for the first step. This work ultimately presents a viable avenue for the carbon-neutral conversion of CH₄ (or other alkanes) to H₂ if a renewable energy resource, such as solar energy, is leveraged to supply process heat.

Role of Surface Oxygen Vacancy Concentration on the Dissociation of Methane over Nonstoichiometric Ceria
Kent Warren and Jonathan R. Scheffe
The Journal of Physical Chemistry C, vol. 123: no. 21, pp. 13208-13218, American Chemical Society, 2019.
external page:
DOI: 10.1021/acs.jpcc.9b01352
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Chemical looping reforming of methane over ceria-based materials is a promising route for the production of synthetic liquid fuel precursors, H₂ and CO. In this work, a comprehensive kinetic model was established, based on thermogravimetric experiments, to describe the previously unresolved, surface-mediated mechanism of methane dissociation via ceria oxygen removal, the first heterogeneous, noncatalytic reaction within the aforementioned redox cycle. Prior studies have suggested that either a surface oxygen anion or vacancy is responsible for the activation of methane. However, here, these two theories are combined to unambiguously show that the prominence of each pathway is dependent on the availability of surface oxygen. Thus, at sufficiently high oxygen nonstoichiometries or for low-surface-area samples, as examined in this work, the vacancy-mediated dissociation of methane is predominant. This assertion was elucidated by mathematically describing a series of rate-determining steps based on surface interactions of known reactive intermediates and fitting the postulated reaction mechanism to temperature-dependent measurements obtained with multistage isothermal thermogravimetry. Corresponding Arrhenius parameters were extracted with excellent agreement between the model predictions and experimentally measured rates. Further validation of the hypothesized reaction mechanism is supported by (1) close similarity between activation energies obtained through model fits and separate isoconversional techniques and (2) expected trends observed with acceptor-doped ceria that has a higher concentration of extrinsic oxygen vacancies at the onset of the reaction. The quantitative kinetic insight obtained from the model presented herein allows for the evaluation and optimization of ceria-based materials in larger-scale, chemical looping processes.

Experimental Framework for Evaluation of the Thermodynamic and Kinetic Parameters of Metal-Oxides for Solar Thermochemical Fuel Production
Richard J. Carrillo, Kent Warren and Jonathan R. Scheffe
Journal of Solar Energy Engineering, vol. 141: no. 2, pp. 021007, American Society of Mechanical Engineers, 2019.
external page:
DOI: 10.1115/1.4042088
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

The two-step metal oxide redox cycle is a promising and thermodynamically attractive means of solar fuel production. In this work, we describe the development of a high-temperature tubular reactor in which the fundamental thermodynamic and kinetic behavior of thermochemical materials can be readily assessed. This reactor system is capable of operating at temperatures up to 1873 K, total pressures ranging from vacuum to ambient, and oxygen partial pressures (pO₂) as low as 10⁻²⁹ atm. Compared to off-the-shelf systems like thermogravimetric analyzers or indirect conductivity-based measurement systems, this system has three inherent benefits: (1) the flexibility to control the sample morphology (e.g., powder, packed bed, reticulated porous ceramic, or pellet), (2) the potential for a well-developed and characterized flow, and (3) the ability to readily customize the system on demand (e.g., easy integration with a steam generator to control and operate at very low pO2). The reactor system and experimental methods were validated by performing isothermal relaxation experiments with undoped ceria, wherein the sample environment was rapidly altered by stepwise changes in the delivered H₂O vapor concentration, and comparing measured oxygen nonstoichiometries with accepted data available in the literature. Data were measured at temperatures from 1173 to 1473 K and pO₂ from 4.54  × 10⁻¹⁸ to 1.02 × 10⁻⁹ atm. The measured equilibrium data displayed strong agreement with the literature and the expected trends were preserved. Kinetic data were extracted by first transforming reactant concentrations measured downstream of the reaction zone using a tanks-in-series mixing model to account for gas dispersion. Next, a mechanistic kinetic model distinguishing surface and bulk species concentrations was fit to the data to extract pertinent thermodynamic and kinetic parameters. The model assumed a two-step reaction mechanism mediated by the formation of an intermediate hydroxyl species on the surface. Activation energies and defect formation enthalpies and entropies for the forward and reverse reactions were found to be in good agreement with previous modeling efforts, providing further validation of the use of this system to explore thermodynamic and kinetic behavior of emerging thermochemical materials.

Kinetic insights into the reduction of ceria facilitated via the partial oxidation of methane
Kent Warren and Jonathan R. Scheffe
Materials Today Energy, vol. 9, pp. 39-48, Elsevier, 2018.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.mtener.2018.05.001
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

A kinetic relaxation study of the partial oxidation of methane over ceria in the absence of gas-phase oxygen, a promising pathway for syngas production, was performed under atmospheric pressure between 750 °C and 1100 °C. Using a thermogravimetric analyzer, the effect of cyclability, gas/solid diffusion, reactant/product composition, and surface contaminants on the measured rate of ceria reduction was addressed. Reaction kinetics were described by an apparent activation energy that was extracted from Arrhenius-type plots as a function of composition. Determined via two isoconversional methods, apparent activation energy was shown to vary with reaction extent between 20 kJ mol⁻¹ and 80 kJ mol⁻¹, increasing mostly for large reduction extents (δ > 0.15). Interestingly, this range is lower than the discrete values presented in literature and supports the idea that reaction rates are not limited by bulk diffusion. Further, although deposited carbon was not experimentally detected, evidence of surface adsorbates, such as carbonates and hydroxyls, was observed, as the reaction rate was impacted by the length of subsequent oxidation in O2. The reaction rate was also proportional to the methane partial pressure and weakly inhibited by additional hydrogen (only at large nonstoichiometries), in agreement with equilibrium thermodynamic predictions. Experimental repeatability upon isothermal cycling increased with increasing operating temperature, contrary to higher-temperature, ceria-based thermochemical cycles. Overall, this work provides new experimental insights that affect the partial oxidation of methane over ceria and help guide the immediate development of larger scale systems.

Theoretical and Experimental Investigation of Solar Methane Reforming through the Nonstoichiometric Ceria Redox Cycle
Kent Warren, Julie Reim, Kelvin Randhir, Benjamin Greek, Richard Carrillo, David W. Hahn and Jonathan R. Scheffe
Energy Technology, vol. 5: no. 11, pp. 2138-2149, Wiley-VCH, 2017.
external page:
DOI: 10.1002/ente.201700083
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

The dry reforming of methane through the nonstoichiometric ceria (CeO₂–CeO₂₋δ) redox cycle was examined theoretically and experimentally for converting high-temperature, solar process heat to syngas. The aforementioned cycle is composed of: (1) endothermic reduction of ceria and simultaneous partial oxidation of methane and (2) exothermic oxidation of the reduced ceria and simultaneous reduction of CO₂. In both steps, chemical equilibrium calculations indicate that isothermal operation is thermodynamically favorable under a wide range of conditions. The influence of the total amount of reactive gas, the operating temperature, and the inclusion of gas- or solid-phase heat exchangers on the cycle performance was determined through a holistic process model. A theoretical solar-to-fuel conversion efficiency, defined as the ratio of the difference between the calorific value of syngas (H₂ and CO) produced and methane converted to the total solar radiative input energy, of more than 45 % was predicted with no heat recuperation. Upon the completion of the reduction at each temperature, the bed-averaged oxygen nonstoichiometry equaled 0.07, 0.21, and 0.24, which yielded methane conversions of 9, 41, and 51 %, respectively. At 1120 °C, the extrapolated solar-to-fuel conversion efficiency was 9.82 %.

Abstract 

Global hydrogen demand currently stands at approximately 100 million metric tons per year and is projected to more than double over the next 15 years as industries such as steelmaking pursue decarbonization. Solar Thermochemical Carbon Dioxide Splitting Using Ceria and Iron Aluminate Foam Devices and Simulation of a Plant System for Demonstration
Mitsuho Nakakura, Yoshiko Koyama, Sofu Shibuya, Kent Warren, Alan Weimer, Tucker Farrell, Tatsunori Asaoka and Koji Matsubara
18th International Conference on Energy Sustainability (ES 2024), Anaheim, CA, USA, pp.ES2024-131100 New York, NY: American Society of Mechanical Engineers, July 15-17, 2024.
external page:
DOI: 10.1115/ES2024-131100
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

An international research project has been undertaken to integrate a unique solar thermal processing reactor system with ceria and iron aluminate as active redox materials for CO₂ splitting. Experimental investigations for CO₂ splitting were conducted using a solar simulator and tube furnace at Niigata University, followed by demonstrations using a high-flux solar furnace (HFSF) at the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) in Golden, CO. Each experimental setup consisted of foam devices composed of reticulated porous ceramic (RPC). The RPC has a full ceria or iron aluminate body. It fabricated using the replica method and subjected to a two-step redox reaction, which iteratively separated a stream of CO₂ into O₂ and CO. Reactivity was evaluated using CO production per mass of the reactive material. The tubular furnace yielded a CO production of 6.41 mL/g at a reduction temperature of 1600°C, showcasing a higher CO production rate and total amount than those obtained from experiments conducted with solar simulators and solar furnace setups. Linnea Helenius, Catherine Trautman, Jess Connell, William Paulson, Alice Ly, Kent Warren and Alan Weimer

Abstract 

Reticulated porous ceramic (RPC) iron aluminate (Fe0.5Al0.5)3O4 parts are created as an active material to thermochemically split water and carbon dioxide. The RPC facilitates this high temperature (1400°C-1600°C) two-step redox cycle by increasing the specific surface area to promote reaction rates and increasing the porosity to allow faster heat transfer by radiation. It has advantages over particle-based reactor designs because it is less prone to channeling and elutriation or diffusion of hot particles to heat-sensitive reactor components. The RPC structures are fabricated via the replication method. A polyurethane foam is cast in iron aluminate slurry and subjected to a thermal treatment to burn out the foam and sinter the part. The morphology of the RPC is characterized by micro-computed tomography, scanning electron microscopy, mercury porosimetry, image analysis, and hydrodynamic experiments to elucidate the specific surface area, porosity, pore diameter, strut diameter, and permeability. Thermochemical performance is evaluated using thermogravimetry.

Enhancing high temperature heat transfer for clean industrial process heating via radiatively participating gases
Madelyn Bennett, Vincent M. Wheeler, Trevor Wilson, Robert Anderson, William Bierzychudek, Dragan Mejic, Kent Warren and Alan W. Weimer
Applied Thermal Engineering, vol. 306, pp. 133157, Elsevier, 2026.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.applthermaleng.2026.133157
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

High-temperature industrial heat demand represents a major source of CO2 emissions. Solar receivers in concentrated solar technology (CST) systems have reached temperatures exceeding 1000 °C, offering a pathway for reducing carbon emissions from high-temperature industrial processes. At these temperatures, thermal radiation becomes an important mode of heat transfer, yet no experimental study has compared radiatively participating (CO2) and non-participating (N2) gases as heat transfer fluids (HTFs), a gap this work addresses with the first such experimental comparison, to the author's knowledge, from 1000 °C to 1400 °C. A high-temperature experimental setup was designed and constructed to measure centerline temperature profiles via an axially driven Type R thermocouple for different CO2 and N2 flow rates. Experimental measurements validated the computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model, which was used to investigate the effects of pipe diameters and Reynolds numbers. Increasing Reynolds numbers showed slightly decreased radiation contribution for a given geometry. Increasing the pipe diameter from 10 mm to 100 mm increased the optical thickness from 0.0405 to 0.405 for the 2.7 μm band and 0.0648 to 0.648 for the 4.3 μm band, leading to stronger radiation absorption for CO2. The radiation contribution to the overall heat flux increased from 3.5% to 66.6% for 10 mm to 100 mm pipe diameters. Larger pipe diameters showed significant radiation absorption for CO2, allowing CO2 to carry more heat than N2 on a mass flow rate basis. These findings demonstrate the potential of radiatively participating gases to improve heat transfer performance in high-temperature industrial processes and CST systems.

Ceria and Iron Aluminate Active Materials for Solar Thermochemical Fuel Production
Kent Warren
10th Forum on New Materials (CIMTEC 2026), Perugia, Italy Faenza: CIMTEC 2026, June 21-24, 2026.
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Widespread access to H2 and CO is critical for global decarbonization, yet producing a clean supply of these synthetic liquid fuel precursors at costs competitive with fossil-based alternatives remains constrained by the limited capacity and durability of incumbent electrolysis technologies. Thermochemical approaches that exploit the oxygen-exchange capacity of metal oxides offer a promising route to overcome these limitations, although challenges persist in identifying oxygen carriers capable of efficient operation within the confines of established chemical processes. In this presentation, benchmark candidates from two distinct classes of nonstoichiometric metal oxides - ceria fluorites and iron aluminate spinels - are examined for their effectiveness in facilitating thermochemical H2O and CO2 splitting. Their redox behavior, governed by oxygen- and cation-vacancy-mediated transport, respectively, is characterized through both closed- and open-system analyses to elucidate the relationship between thermodynamic properties and performance. The implications of these findings are discussed in the context of process integration, illustrating how the rational design of materials with tailored redox properties is poised to enable commercially viable routes to sustainable fuels.

Particle atomic layer deposition in fluidized bed reactors: An updated perspective on reactor design and low-pressure fluidization
Davis R. Conklin, Hailey C. Loehde-Woolard, Julie A. Nguyen, Michael A. Zaza, Bergen Evans, William M. Paulson, Hermann F. Klein-Hessling Barrientos, Mark Wallace, Robert L. Anderson, Dragan Mejic, Kent Warren and Alan W. Weimer
Powder Technology, vol. 476, pp. 122364, Elsevier, 2026.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.powtec.2026.122364
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Atomic layer deposition (ALD) on particle substrates has become widely utilized over the last two decades. The ALD fluidized bed reactor (ALD-FBR) is a reactor design popularized by work at the University of Colorado; their work with lab-scale reactor systems has produced fundamental learnings for the field, including the capability of ALD to coat primary nanoparticles. As the applications of particle ALD continue to expand, so do the complexity of ALD chemistry and powder processing. To maintain a high level of knowledge and reproducibility across systems, we detail several aspects of ALD-FBRs that have achieved success with challenging precursor or substrate requirements and/or particle fluidization characteristics. Here we discuss considerations for precursor manifolds, precursor introductions to the bed, and air-sensitive materials. In addition, practices for particle fluidization under low pressure (<130 hPa) are discussed with a focus on cohesive powders.

Three-dimensional porous carbons via direct ink writing of bio-sourced precursors, thermal processing, and response surface methodology
Katarina Odak, Kent Warren, Nathan A. Smith, Adrian Gestos, James E. Smay, David E. Clough and Alan W. Weimer
Carbon Trends, vol. 23, pp. 100654, Elsevier, 2026.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.cartre.2026.100654
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

This study presents a manufacturing and processing route for fabricating CO₂-activated three-dimensional porous carbon (3D-PC) materials from microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) and kraft lignin (L), leveraging direct ink writing and controlled thermal processing. Response surface methodology (RSM) was employed to optimize ink formulations based on print shape fidelity, assessed via structured-light 3D scanning in an alternative, high-throughput approach to conventional rheological testing. Printed lignocellulosic structures underwent a three-day thermal schedule under inert conditions, with extended dwell in the 100 260 C range to promote cross-linking of the thermolabile lignin component and char formation from the cellulosic fraction. This process yielded porous carbon architectures with developed internal surface area and sufficient handling robustness for post-processing. A second RSM framework was applied to fine-tune CO₂ activation parameters, enhancing microporosity through selective carbon removal via the Boudouard reaction at defect sites. Nitrogen adsorption isotherms (196 C) confirmed the development of micropores (< 2 nm) without significant geometric degradation, indicating controlled activation rather than unselective gasification. Cold field-emission scanning electron microscopy (CFE-SEM) revealed interconnected meso/macroporosity (> 2 nm), while atomic force microscopy (AFM) confirmed sub-nanometer surface features. This study presents an integrated approach for producing high surface-area porous carbons from simple, bio-sourced, water-compatible precursors through direct ink writing, carbonization, and CO₂ activation, supporting the scalable design of architected porous carbon materials and establishing a manufacturing framework relevant to applications such as energy storage, gas adsorption, thermal insulation, and catalysis.

Cation Vacancies Mediate Thermochemical Water Splitting with Iron Aluminates
Nathan J. Szymanski, Kent Warren, Alan W. Weimer and Christopher J. Bartel
Chemistry of Materials, vol. 38: no. 7, pp. 3145-3156, American Chemical Society, 2026.
external page:
DOI: 10.1021/acs.chemmater.5c02365
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Solar thermochemical water splitting enables hydrogen production by cycling metal oxides between reduced and oxidized states, typically through an oxygen vacancy mechanism. However, recent experimental work suggests that cation vacancies have a greater influence on the redox behavior of iron aluminate spinels used in water splitting. This remains debated, as calculations predict that such cation vacancies are thermodynamically unfavorable. In the current work, we show that Fe vacancies in (FeζAl₁−ζ)₃O₄ become accessible only when facilitated by inversion between Fe and Al. This antisite disorder lowers the formation energy of octahedral Fe vacancies in Al-rich spinels (ζ = 1/3) from over 3 eV to just 0.62 eV when one-third of the cation sites are inverted, allowing high Fe vacancy concentrations under oxidizing conditions. This mechanism supports high H₂ yields up to 361 μmol/g, consistent with experimental observations. Our findings support the notion that solar thermochemical water splitting can occur through a cation vacancy mechanism. They also clarify how site inversion, vacancy energetics, and defect interactions each contribute to redox performance, offering general design principles for identifying and optimizing materials that operate through cation vacancy cycling.

Accelerating activated sintering of tungsten with palladium particle atomic layer deposition
Davis R. Conklin, Hailey C. Loehde-Woolard, Michael Zaza, Bergen Evans, Arne Croell, Martin Volz, Kent Warren, Rishi Raj, Jhonathan Rosales and Alan W. Weimer
International Journal of Refractory Metals & Hard Materials, vol. 135, pp. 107554, Elsevier, 2026.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.ijrmhm.2025.107554
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Tungsten refractory metal parts are produced by sintering metal powder compacts well above 2000 °C. Transition metal additives (e.g. Ni, Pd) can be deposited on the surfaces of the tungsten particles prior to sintering to create a sub-nanometer intergranular film that accelerates diffusional mass transport and resulting sintering kinetics to allow sintering below 1500 °C. The uniformity of the additives is known to influence sintering performance, but prior sintering aid studies for tungsten utilize solution impregnation techniques that limit control over additive distribution. In this work, we employ particle atomic layer deposition (ALD) to deposit a highly uniform Pd coating on tungsten powder and demonstrate improved activated sintering kinetics relative to Pd deposited by solution impregnation. Both techniques were used to deposit about 0.1 wt% Pd, but an increase in powder surface area during the solution impregnation process reduced the surface-specific Pd loading relative to ALD-coated tungsten. The ALD technique also reduced oxygen and carbon contamination and produced superior Pd dispersion. As a result, the ALD Pd-W powder began sintering about 100 °C below the impregnated powder and exhibited a lower activation energy for densification. Although this produces minor microstructural differences in powders sintered under typical hours-long heating cycles, we anticipate that this approach will enable shorter, cooler, and ultimately less expensive processes for tungsten powder metallurgy. This is the first application of particle ALD for powder metallurgy, demonstrating a promising approach to incorporate dilute additives in instances where uniformity and composition control are critical to processing and performance.

Technoeconomic analysis of the modular valorization of flare gas to carbon additives for concrete
Jessica A. Hauck, Theodore A. Champ, Kent Warren, Andrew Broerman, Boning Wang, Mija H. Hubler and Alan W. Weimer
Energy Conversion and Management, vol. 348, pp. 120783, Elsevier, 2026.
external page:
DOI: 10.1016/j.enconman.2025.120783
external page:
Research Collection
Abstract 

Despite widespread efforts to mitigate emissions, significant volumes of natural gas continue to be flared due to limited infrastructure and the absence of mobile systems for on-site utilization. A promising approach is a modular system designed to valorize flare gas by converting natural gas into carbon nanomaterials for use in concrete. Unlike conventional methane pyrolysis which prioritizes hydrogen as the primary product, this design proposes using hydrogen as a heat source and instead targets carbon, the dominant product by mass, as the economic driver. The system employs a fluidized bed chemical vapor deposition reactor with a sacrificial catalyst composed of iron deposited onto silica fume. This process yields a composite carbon nanoproduct containing carbon nanoparticles and nanofilaments deposited on the catalyst, eliminating the need for product separation or catalyst regeneration. The modular unit is designed for remote deployment and includes integrated sulfur removal, requiring only natural gas as the thermal input. Technoeconomic analysis shows that the levelized selling price of the solid product remains below $7 per kilogram across a range of catalyst loadings and carbon yields, with carbon nanoproduct values ranging from $14 to $62 per kilogram, not including the cost of carbon–catalyst separation. The product can be incorporated into cement at 0.25 wt% loading with less than a 10 % increase in cost, enabling applications in high-performance concrete. While further experimental validation and design optimization are warranted, this study demonstrates a novel and viable concept for converting flared gas into functional carbon additives for concrete.
